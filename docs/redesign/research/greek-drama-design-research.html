Start with a work that has a face

Homer has the strongest sense of a particular work: the large Greek title, illustration, and passage context give the page a purpose before reading starts. Aristotle puts more controls and dense navigation ahead of the text. The lesson for plays is to keep Homer’s identity, then make the reading state quieter.

The repo already has speaker colors, shared lines, chorus passages, stage directions, and translation comparison. Its earlier notes already explored theatre-programme, manuscript, and score treatments. The new work should sharpen the reading behavior and test different layouts, rather than rename those old directions.

I reviewed the public readers and local source/design notes. I did not change the repo or run its current app locally. The Homer and Aristotle screenshots show their public sites, not the current Greek Plays build.

A home page for four playwrights

You want the playwrights to carry the home page, with large illustrated features closer to Homer. The current page puts search, lexicon, support, and starter cards before the authors. Give the authors the main space, with search in the header and a small Resume link near the top.

Home: four large illustrated features

Use two columns on a wide screen and stack them on phones. Each playwright gets a prominent Greek name, a clear English name, a distinct drawing, a play count, and an obvious author-page link. Two or three direct play links can help someone start reading quickly. Give all four equal prominence; no carousel or hover-only discovery.

The image subjects can come from the playwright’s work rather than uncertain portraits: an Oresteia scene for Aeschylus; Oedipus and the Sphinx for Sophocles; a directly sourced scene for Euripides; a comic actor or checked comedy reference for Aristophanes. These are source directions, not four final image choices.

Author pages: illustrated titles without boxes

Use a compact author heading, then a clear title column with each play’s drawing beside it. Keep the Greek title close, with one short premise and useful verified metadata. Let the images vary in shape within a common area. Avoid thumbnail squares, shadows, rounded containers, and borders around every entry.

Keep all titles visible by default. Offer a compact titles-only view that remembers the choice. A small Find a play field helps with Euripides’ 19 titles; the seven-play pages should not need a large filter toolbar.

Aeschylus’ Oresteia can receive a small group treatment for its three plays. Other grouping needs an honest basis. In particular, do not present Sophocles’ Theban plays as a composed trilogy. Preserve the registry’s order as the default and keep attribution notices.

Ask Claude to show the entire visit: home → playwright → play opening → reading page. Compare Euripides’ long list with Aeschylus’ short list. A redesign that only improves the play opening leaves the first two pages unfinished.

Make the play’s form useful

The lasting interest should come from changes in the text and useful control over the view. A reader will value those changes on the fourth visit, when a decorative opening has become familiar.

Let a speech be the main reading group

Use a shared speaker gutter beside both languages. Give the name a strong first appearance, then quiet continuation marks for long speeches. Tighten space for rapid exchanges; give long speeches a calm measure. Keep the first baselines of matched groups aligned, without creating huge gaps after every translated verse.

Launch refinement · shared-line logic already exists.

Make the cast list follow the reading task

Offer a narrow, optional cast list with name, role, and speaker mark. Let a click move to that character’s next speech, or show a list of their passages. The current speaker can stay visible when the start of a long speech scrolls away.

Call this a speaker list. Do not label it “Onstage now” unless you have checked entrances and exits: a person may stay present while silent, and some voices come from inside.

Speaker navigation can use existing data · presence tracking needs editorial data.

Distinguish speech, song, and shared lament

Use narrower measure and stanza space for verified lyric passages. Keep the same fonts and restrained speaker marks. Joint laments need both the lyric grouping and changing voices. A chorus can speak; a solo character can sing. Avoid equating “Chorus” with “song.”

Refine existing chorus styling now · verify form metadata before adding new labels.

Give phones a translation choice that holds their place

Show Greek with an expandable translation for each speech, or one language with a switch tied to the same citation. Offer full parallel mode where width allows it. Keep the selected translator visible. On desktop, translation comparison can remain two or three plain columns.

Launch candidate · test long speeches and shared lines, not just the opening.

Make return visits start at the text

A saved citation should lead straight to Resume. Keep Start and About this play close by. Restore the language mode and translator. The opening can still contain title, object image, cast, and sources, but it should not become a repeated obstacle.

Put the full synopsis behind a clear spoiler label. The current plate renders an argument and a synopsis, so this matters before launch.

Launch candidate · store the citation, not only a scroll offset.

Use structural navigation with evidence

A compact list can jump to a verified prologue, entrance song, episode, ode, or exit passage. Include line ranges and optional short, spoiler-aware descriptions. Until those divisions are checked, line jumps and speaker passages are honest and useful.

Later editorial work if the required divisions are absent.

Keep parsing close without shifting the page

Use a stable side panel on a wide screen and a bottom sheet on a phone. Keep the word highlighted and make return to reading immediate. A long dictionary entry should scroll inside the panel rather than stretch the speech. Translation, cast, and notes should not all compete for the same margin.

Launch refinement · preserve existing parsing and keyboard behavior.

Do not add speech bubbles, cards around every turn, a simulated stage, or changing ornaments for repeat visits. The strongest distinction from a treatise reader is a clear view of voices, verse, song, and translation.

Real theater objects, at useful image sizes

These general theater objects guide the site’s style. Each play should receive a separate image tied to its own story, as planned below. Keep photos away from the reading surface. A caption can explain the link to theater without pretending that a generic mask portrays a character in the chosen play.

Pronomos vase

Attic red-figure volute krater, late fifth century BCE, Naples, MANN 81673. Actors, masks, music, and satyr-play company make this the richest theater reference here. The Getty artist record describes its theatrical subject.

Design use: study the cast grouping and repeated figures. The full photograph has reflections and a busy background; a small crop must still show enough to make sense. The engraving below offers a clearer view of the decoration.

Terracotta comic mask

Greek, Cypriot; late fourth–early third century BCE. Met 74.51.1520. A miniature, about 4.8 cm high, associated with a New Comedy youth type. It is not a surviving full-size mask worn by a Classical actor.

Design use: its pale face, dark mouth, and uneven contour make a strong small image beside a title. The image stays legible at modest size. Use the actual object rather than a stock comedy/tragedy symbol.

Terracotta actor statuette

Greek; late fifth–early fourth century BCE. Met 13.225.22. About 9.8 cm high; a bearded figure with a basket. The Met places it among early actor statuettes and notes their original bright paint.

Design use: a full figure gives gesture and character without making the entire site a mask theme. Good for a small opening image or a carefully drawn outline based on the source.

Head, possibly of a comic actor

Greek, South Italian, Tarentine; fourth century BCE. Met 10.210.137. About 6.35 cm high. Keep the museum’s uncertainty about the identification.

Design use: the worn surface and bold brows offer a less polished, less generic reference. Study the shape and scale; do not use a distressed texture behind Greek text.

Pronomos vase, 1841 engraving

The broad image makes the figures and masks easier to study than the curved vase photograph. It also offers a source for the kind of line drawing you like in Homer. A low-contrast full-width strip will lose detail; choose a few clear figures, keep a link to the complete plate, and credit any adaptation.

For assets you intend to ship, start with the Met’s public-domain images. The two Commons files carry CC BY-SA terms: credit the named contributor, link the license, and mark changes. Their file pages also provide further source and museum-use notes. Old objects do not make every photograph public domain.

The Met image links above deliver 900–1200 px images. Use each object record’s download option if production needs a larger original. The Commons links deliver the full stated resolutions.

A distinct image for each play

The landing pages need 44 distinct images, each linked to its play. A shared drawing style can tie them together without repeating the same mask or figure. You chose drawings based on ancient objects. Use a shared drawing style across all 44 images, keeping the source beside each image.

Source in this order: an ancient depiction tied to the play; an identified depiction of its myth or central figure; a clearly labeled thematic object; then a new illustration based on checked references. A myth scene is not automatically evidence of a performance or the exact surviving play. A modern illustration must not masquerade as an ancient artifact.

Medea: an actual play-related image

This Lucanian krater, about 400 BCE, shows Medea in a dragon-drawn chariot. Cleveland explicitly links the scene to Euripides. It offers a much stronger source than an anonymous mask. A final crop or drawing should consider whether to show the ending.

Museum record and original download.

Iphigenia at Aulis: choose a scene

This second-century BCE Boeotian bowl depicts five scenes from the play, with figure inscriptions. It gives a direct source for a distinct image. Inspect the other museum views before selecting the arrival, letter, or another scene: the relief is faint in this frontal view.

Ask Claude for three different reading studies

Use the same play and passage in each. Ask for an opening, a mid-play desktop page, and a phone page. Reject three versions that only change the accent color.

1. Object-based drawing at the opening

A distinct drawing based on a sourced object next to the title; a compact cast and source note; then plain bilingual speech groups with a shared speaker gutter. This is my preferred first study. It carries Homer’s identity into the play reader while keeping the body quiet.

Starting tokens: pale neutral background #F5F5F2; ink #22252B; muted text #626970; divider #D6D8D8; one accent #6D385A. Keep Cardo for reading and titles at first. Use the existing control face. Limit the opening image to roughly one third of the plate; no image under body text. These are study choices, not measured final contrast approvals.

2. Speakers first

A stronger speaker gutter, optional cast navigation, and a compact heading. Use the existing speaker marks and colors sparingly. Make a rapid exchange look different from a messenger speech through spacing and name placement. Test whether the extra cast column is worth the space it costs.

Key comparison: can the reader track five speaker changes without scanning both language columns for names?

3. Greek first

A larger Greek measure with speech-level translation reveal, plus a clear switch to full parallel or comparison mode. Keep the citation and speaker in view when the translation opens. On a phone this may become the strongest default, but test it with actual users and long passages.

Key comparison: does reveal reduce clutter, or does it make the reader tap too much?

None needs a fake antique surface, an animated theater, or a generic “explore the timeless world” introduction. Names, line references, text form, and a well-chosen real object can give the site its identity.

Avoid the LLM design defaults

A pale background and a good serif can work here—you already like Homer. They do not finish the design. The specific drawings, strong author features, useful play lists, and careful reading layout should make the site recognizably yours.

Desktop hover: slight and local

The hover effect remains open for design exploration. A drawing moving upward about 3px over 180–220ms is one candidate; a slight contrast change or a small reveal within the illustration may suit the artwork better. Ask Opus to compare two or three restrained options. Keep the title, metadata, play links, and page geometry still. Treat the feature as one hover area so crossing its parts does not restart the effect. Do not add a tilt, shadow lift, image swap, or moving description.

Use the motion only for a fine pointer that supports hover. Reduced-motion users get a static cue; keyboard users get clear focus; touch users see the complete page without hover. Keep direct play links separate from the author link so each click has the expected destination.

Review the whole design without motion. If it could serve any museum or book site after swapping four names, the drawings and composition need more work. Hover adds a small response; it should not supply the page’s identity.
