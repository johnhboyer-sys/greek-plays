THE FROGS

[SCENE—the front of Hercules' temple.]

XANTHIAS. SHALL I say some of the usual jokes, master, at which the spectators always laugh.[2]

BACCHUS. Yes, by Jove, whatever you please, except “I am burdened;”[3] but beware of this, for it is by this time utterly sickening to me.[4]

XANTHIAS. Nor any thing else facetious?

BACCHUS. Except, "How I am afflicted!"

XANTHIAS. What then? shall I say what is very laughable?

BACCHUS. Aye, by Jove, boldly: that thing only [5] take care you say not—

XANTHIAS. What?

BACCHUS. That with shifting the yoke [6] from one shoulder to the other, you desire to ease yourself.

XANTHIAS. Nor that I shall break wind with carrying so great a load upon me, unless some one shall remove it?

BACCHUS. Nay, do not, I beseech you, except when I am about to vomit.

XANTHIAS. Then what occasion [7] was there that I should carry this baggage, if I am to do none of those things which Phrynichus [8] is accustomed to do, and Lycis, and Amipsias? They are always carrying baggage in Comedy. [9]

BACCHUS. Don't do so then; for whenever, being a spectator, I see any of these stage tricks, I come away older by more than a year. [10]

XANTHIAS. O this thrice-unlucky neck then! because it is distressed, but must not utter what is laughable.

BACCHUS. Then is not this insolence and much conceit, when I, who am Bacchus, son of—a wine-jar,[11] am walking myself, and toiling, while I let him ride, in order that he might not be[12] distressed or carry a burden?

XANTHIAS. Why, do I not carry?

BACCHUS. Why, how do you carry, who are carried?

XANTHIAS. Because I carry these.[13]

BACCHUS. In what way?

XANTHIAS. Very heavily.

BACCHUS. Does not the ass then carry this weight which you carry?

XANTHIAS. Certainly not what I hold and carry; no, by Jove!

BACCHUS. Why, how do you carry, who are yourself carried by another?

XANTHIAS. I know not; but this shoulder of mine is burdened.

BACCHUS. Do you then, since you deny that the ass assists you, in your turn take up and carry the ass.

XANTHIAS. Ah me, miserable! Why was I not at the sea-fight?[14] Of a truth I would have bid a long farewell to you.[15]

BACCHUS. Dismount, you scoundrel, for now I go[16] near this door, whither I was first to betake myself. [Knocks violently at the door.] Little boy, boy, I say,[17] boy! [Xanthias dismounts from his ass.]

HERCULES. (from within). Who knocked at the door? How Centaur-like[18] he rushed at it, whoever he is. [The door opens, and Hercules comes out.] Tell me, what's this?[19]

BACCHUS. (addressing Xanthias). Boy![20]

XANTHIAS. What's the matter?

BACCHUS. Did you not observe?

XANTHIAS. What?

BACCHUS. How exceedingly he was afraid of me.

XANTHIAS. Yes, by Jove, lest you should be mad.

HERCULES. (aside). By Ceres, I certainly am not able[21] to refrain from laughing, though I bite my lips; nevertheless I laugh.

BACCHUS. My good sir, come forward; for I have some need of you.

HERCULES. (trying to suppress his laughter). I am not able to drive away my laughter, when I see a lion's skin lying upon a saffron-coloured robe.[22] What's your purpose? Why
have the buskin and club[23] come together? Whither in the world have you been abroad?

BACCHUS. I embarked on board the Clisthenes.[24]

HERCULES. And fought at sea?

BACCHUS. And we sunk either twelve or thirteen ships of the enemy too.[25]

HERCULES. You two?

BACCHUS. Yea, by Apollo!

HERCULES. "And then I awoke."[26]

BACCHUS. And indeed, as I was reading the Andromeda to myself[27] on board the ship, suddenly a desire smote my heart, you can't think how vehemently.[28]

HERCULES. Desire? How great[29] a one?

BACCHUS. A little one: as big as Molon.[30]

HERCULES. For a woman?

BACCHUS. Certainly not.

HERCULES. For a boy, then?[31]

BACCHUS. By no means.

HERCULES. For a man, then?

BACCHUS. Faugh!

HERCULES. Have you been with Clisthenes?

BACCHUS. Do not mock me, brother, for[32] I am distressed; such a desire utterly undoes me.

HERCULES. Of what sort, my little brother?

BACCHUS. I am not able to tell it; yet certainly[33] will I declare it to you in a riddle.[34] Did you ever[35] suddenly desire pea-soup?

HERCULES. Pea-soup? bless me! ten thousand times in my life.

BACCHUS. Shall I teach you thoroughly the truth[36] of the matter, or shall I declare it in some other way?

HERCULES. Nay, do not about the pea-soup at least; for I understand that instance very well.

BACCHUS. Therefore such a longing for Euripides consumes me—

HERCULES. And that too[37] when he is dead?

BACCHUS. And no man could persuade me, so as not to go to fetch him.[38]

HERCULES. To Hades below?

BACCHUS. Aye, and, by Jove, lower still, if there be aught still lower.[39]

HERCULES. With[40] what intent?

BACCHUS. I want a clever poet, “for[41] some are no longer alive, and others who are living, are bad.”

HERCULES. What then, is not[42] Iophon alive?

BACCHUS. Why, to be[43] sure this is even the only good thing still remaining, if indeed even this be good ; for I don’t know[44] for certain even how this is.

HERCULES. Do you not mean, then, to bring up Sophocles,[45] who is before[46] Euripides, if you must bring one from thence?

BACCHUS. Not before I shall have taken Iophon alone by himself, and tried him, what he can do without Sophocles. And besides, Euripides, as he is roguish, would even attempt to run away hither along with me, while the other is easy here, and easy there.[47]

HERCULES. But where’s Agathon?[48]

BACCHUS. He has left me and gone, a good [49] poet, and much regretted by his friends.[50]

HERCULES. Whither in the world is the poor fellow [51] gone?

BACCHUS. To the banquet of the blest.

HERCULES. And Xenocles?[52]

BACCHUS. By Jove, may he perish utterly.

HERCULES. And Pythangelus?[53]

XANTHIAS. (aside). But no account [54] made of me, though I am so dreadfully galled in my shoulder.

HERCULES. Are there not therefore here more than ten thousand other mere lads who compose tragedies, more loquacious [55] than Euripides by more than a stadium?[56]

BACCHUS. These are small fry, and chatter-boxes, "twittering-places of the swallows,"[57] disgraces to the art, who vanish speedily, if only they receive a chorus, after having once piddled upon tragedy.[58] But a poet of creative powers you could no longer find,[59] if you searched, who uttered a noble expression.

HERCULES. How creative?

BACCHUS. So creative as to utter[60] some such venturous phrase as “Æther, little mansion[61] of Jove,” or “Foot of time,” or “The mind which was not willing to swear by the victims, and the tongue which swore apart from the mind.”

HERCULES. Do these please you?

BACCHUS. Nay, but[62] they please me to more than madness.

HERCULES. Of a surety[63] they are knavish tricks, as appears even to you.

BACCHUS. Do not direct[64] my mind; for you have a house of your own.

HERCULES. And yet absolutely they appear most villanous.

BACCHUS. Teach me to dine.[65]

XANTHIAS. (aside). But no account of me.

BACCHUS. But tell me these, for the sake of which[66] I have come with this dress, in imitation of you, that you might tell me your entertainers, if I should want them, whom you made use of at that time when you went to fetch Cerberus, the harbours, bakers’ shops, brothels, resting-places, lodging-houses, springs, roads, cities, rooms, hostesses, and where there are fewest bugs.

XANTHIAS. (aside). But no account of me.

HERCULES. Oh rash! why, will you dare to go?

BACCHUS. And do you too say nothing further to this, but tell me about the roads, how we may soonest arrive at Hades below; and tell me neither a hot nor a very cold way.

HERCULES. Come now, which of them shall I tell you first? Which?[67] for there is one way by a rope and a bench, if you hang yourself.

BACCHUS. Have done, you tell me a choking one.

HERCULES. But there is a compendious and well-beaten[68] path, that through a mortar.

BACCHUS. Do you mean hemlock?

HERCULES. Certainly.

BACCHUS. Aye, a cold and chilly one, for it immediately benumbs[69] the shins.

HERCULES. Would you have me tell you a speedy and down-hill road?

BACCHUS. Yes, by Jove, for I am not good at walking.

HERCULES. Creep down then to the Ceramicus.[70]

BACCHUS. And what then?

HERCULES. When you have mounted on the lofty tower—

BACCHUS. What must I do?

HERCULES. Look out thence for the torch to be thrown down; and then, when the spectators call to fling it, do you, too, fling[71] yourself—

BACCHUS. Whither?

HERCULES. Down.

BACCHUS. But I should destroy the two membranes[72] of my brain: I could not travel this way.

HERCULES. What then?

BACCHUS. That whereby you then descended.[73]

HERCULES. But the voyage is long; for you will immediately come to a large lake, altogether bottomless.

BACCHUS. How then shall I get across?

HERCULES. An old sailor-man will carry you over in a little boat only so big, when he has received two obols[74] as his fare.

BACCHUS. Ha! what a mighty power the two obols have every where! How came they thither, too?[75]

HERCULES. Theseus brought them. After this you will see snakes, and innumerable wild beasts most dreadful.

BACCHUS. Do not try[76] to astound, or put me in a fright, for you will not dissuade me.

HERCULES. Then you will see abundant mud,[77] and ever-flowing ordure ; and[78] people lying in this, if any where any one has ever wronged his guest,[79] or appropriated the wages of prostitution, or beaten his mother, or struck his father’s cheek, or sworn a false oath, or if any have transcribed a passage of Morsimus.[80]

BACCHUS. Yea, by the gods, in addition to these also there ought to have been, if any one learnt the Pyrrhic dance of Cinesias.[81]

HERCULES. After that the breath of flutes shall encompass you, and you shall see a most beautiful light, as here,[82] and myrtle groves, and happy bands of men and women,[83] and abundant clapping of hands.

BACCHUS. But who, pray, are these?

HERCULES. The initiated—[84]

XANTHIAS. (aside). By Jove, I am certainly the ass[85] that carries the mystic implements. But I will not hold these any[86] longer. [Throws his baggage on the ground.]

HERCULES. —who will tell you every thing whatever you want. For they dwell[87] very near along the very road, by Pluto’s gates. And now fare you well, brother.[88] [Hercules goes in and shuts the door.]

BACCHUS. Yea, by Jove, and fare you well also; but do you (to Xanthias) take up the baggage again.

XANTHIAS. Before I have laid them down even?

BACCHUS. Aye, and very quickly, let me tell you!

XANTHIAS. Nay, do not, I beseech you, but hire some one of those who are being carried forth to burial, who is going on this errand.[89]

BACCHUS. But if I should not be able?

XANTHIAS. Then let me take[90] them. [A funeral procession with a dead body on a bier crosses the stage.]

BACCHUS. You say well; for they are carrying forth[91] some dead man here.[92] Hollo you! You, I say! you,[93] the dead man! Fellow, will you carry some small baggage to Hades?

DEAD MAN. About[94] how many?

BACCHUS. These here.

DEAD MAN. Will you pay two drachmæ[95] as my pay?

BACCHUS. No, by Jove, but less.

DEAD MAN. (to the bearers). Go you slowly on your way.[96]

BACCHUS. Stay, my good sir, if I may possibly make[97] a bargain with you.

DEAD MAN. Unless you will pay two drachmæ, don't talk.

BACCHUS. Take nine obols.

DEAD MAN. Then may I come to life again! [Funeral procession moves on.]

XANTHIAS. How[98] haughty the accursed fellow is! Won't he smart for it? I'll go myself. [Takes up the baggage again.]

BACCHUS. You are a good and noble fellow. Let us go to the boat. [Here the scene changes[99] to the banks of the Styx.]

CHARON. Avast![100] put to shore!

BACCHUS. By Jove, this[101] is that lake of which he was telling us; and I see a boat too.

XANTHIAS. Aye, by Neptune, and see here’s Charon too!

BACCHUS. Hail, Charon! hail, Charon! hail, Charon!

CHARON. Who is bound to the resting-place from miseries[102] and troubles? who to the plain of Lethe, or to an ass-shearing,[103] or to the Cerberians,[104] or to the crows, or to Tænarus?[105]

BACCHUS. I.

CHARON. Get on board quickly.

BACCHUS. Where d'ye think you shall put[106] in? to the crows really?

CHARON. Yes, by Jove, as far as you are concerned.[107] Now, get on board.

Bac. There. [Makes a silly motion with his hands.]

CHARON. Don't be playing the fool,[113] but row stoutly with your feet against the stretcher.[114]

BACCHUS. Why, how then shall I be able to row, being inexperienced and unused to the sea, and no Salaminian?

CHARON. Very easily; for you shall hear most delightful melodies, as soon as you once lay to your oar.

BACCHUS. From whom?

CHARON. From swans, the frogs, wondrous ones.

BACCHUS. Now give the time!

CHARON. Yeo ho! yeo ho!

FROGS. Brekekekex, coax, coax, brekekekex, coax, coax. Marshy offspring of the fountains, let us utter an harmonious strain of hymns, my sweet-sounding song, coax, coax, which we sung in Limnæ[115] around the Nysæan[116] Bacchus, son of Jove, when the crowd of people rambling about in drunken revelry on the sacred festival of the Chytræ, marched through my demesne. Brekekekex, coax, coax.

BACCHUS. I begin to have a pain in my bottom, you coax, coax. But you, no doubt,[117] don't care.

FROGS. Brekekekex, coax, coax.

BACCHUS. May you perish then together with your coax;[118] for you are nothing else but coax.

FROGS. Aye, justly, you busybody, for the Muses with
beautiful lyre, and horn-footed[119] Pan, who plays reed-sounded strains, have loved me, and the harper Apollo is still more delighted with me on account of the reed,[120] which, put under the lyre, living in the water, I nourish in marshes. Brekekekex, coax, coax.

BACCHUS. I have blisters, and my hinder-end has been sweating this long while, and then presently it will stoop and say "brekekekex, coax, coax." Come, O song-loving race, have done!

FROGS. Nay, rather, we will sing the more, if ever on[121] sunny days we have leapt through galingal and sedge, delighting in strains of song with many a dive; or at the bottom, avoiding the rain of Jove, have chanted[122] our varied watery choral music amid the noise of bursting bubbles. Brekekekex, coax, coax.

BACCHUS. (striking at them and splashing with his oar). I’ll take[123] this from you.

FROGS. Then in truth we shall suffer dreadful things.

BACCHUS. But I more dreadful things, if I shall burst with rowing.

FROGS. Brekekekex, coax, coax.

BACCHUS. A plague take you! for I don't care.

FROGS. Nay, assuredly, we will screech as loud as our throats can compass,[124] throughout the day, brekekekex, coax, coax.

BACCHUS. In this you shall not conquer me.

FROGS. Nor, assuredly, shall you us by any means.[125]

BACCHUS. Never shall you conquer me, for I will screech brekekekex, coax, coax, even if I must all the day, till I overcome your coax. [The frogs suddenly cease croaking.] I thought I should[126] make you cease from your coax at last.

CHARON. Have done! have done! put the boat to land with the oar; step out; pay your fare.

BACCHUS. Take[127] now the two obols. [Bacchus steps out and Charon pushes off again.]

BACCHUS. Xanthias![128] where’s Xanthias? ho, Xanthias![129]

XANTHIAS. (from a distance). Hollo![130]

BACCHUS. Come hither. [Enter Xanthias.]

XANTHIAS. Welcome, master.[131]

BACCHUS. What’s the state of things there?[132]

XANTHIAS. Darkness and mud.

BACCHUS. Then did you see any where there the parricides and the perjured, of whom he spoke to us?

XANTHIAS. And did not you?

BACCHUS. Aye, by Neptune, did I; and now, too, I see them. [Turns and looks towards the audience.] Come now, what shall we do?

XANTHIAS. It is best for us to go forward, for this is the place where he was saying the dreadful wild beasts were.

BACCHUS. How he shall smart for it![133] He was humbugging, so that I might be frightened, as he knew me to be valiant, out of jealousy; for there is nothing so self-conceited as Hercules. But I should wish to fall in with one, and meet with an encounter worthy of my journey.

XANTHIAS. Well now,[134] by Jove, I hear some noise.

BACCHUS. (in a great fright). Where, where is it?

XANTHIAS. From behind.

BACCHUS. Go behind.

XANTHIAS. But it is in front.

BACCHUS. Then go in front.

XANTHIAS. Well now, by Jove, I see a huge wild beast.

BACCHUS. What sort of a one?

XANTHIAS. Dreadful: at any rate it becomes of every shape; at one time an ox, and now a mule, and at another time, again, a most beautiful woman.

BACCHUS. Where is she? come, let me go to her.

XANTHIAS. But, again, it is no longer a woman, but now it is a dog.

BACCHUS. Then it is the Empusa.[135]

XANTHIAS. At any rate her whole face blazes with fire.

BACCHUS. And she has a brazen leg.

XANTHIAS. Aye, by Neptune, and the other, be well assured, is that of an ass.[136]

BACCHUS. Whither then can I betake myself?

XANTHIAS. And whither I?

BACCHUS. (runs to the front of the stage). O priest,[137] preserve me, that I may be your boon companion.

XANTHIAS. We shall perish, O king Hercules.

BACCHUS. Don't call me,[138] fellow, I beseech you, or pronounce my[139] name.

XANTHIAS. O Bacchus, then.

BACCHUS. This name still less than the other.

XANTHIAS. Go where you are going. Hither, hither, master!

BACCHUS. What's the matter?

XANTHIAS. Be of good courage: we are altogether prosperous,[140] and we may say, like Hegelochus,[141] "for after the billows again I see a calm." The Empusa is gone.

BACCHUS. Swear it.

XANTHIAS. By Jove.

BACCHUS. And swear again.

XANTHIAS. By Jove.

BACCHUS. Swear.

XANTHIAS. By Jove.

BACCHUS. Ah me, miserable! how pale I grew at the sight of her!

XANTHIAS. But this fellow in his fright turned redder than I.

BACCHUS. Ah me! Whence have these evils befallen me? Whom of the gods shall I accuse of ruining me? “Æther, little mansion of Jove,” or, “Foot of time?” [A distant sound of flute-music is heard from behind the scenes.]

XANTHIAS. Hollo!

BACCHUS. What’s the matter?

XANTHIAS. Did you not hear?

BACCHUS. What?

XANTHIAS. The breath of flutes.

BACCHUS. I did; and a very mystical[142] odour of torches too breathed upon me. Come, let us crouch down softly and listen. [Bacchus and Xanthias retire to one side.]

On the other side the word-making, polished tongue, examiner of words, twisting about, agitating envious jaws, dissecting the words of his opponent, will refine away to nothing vast labour of the lungs.[379] [Enter Bacchus, Pluto, Æschylus, and Euripides.]

EURIPIDES. I will not give up[380] the seat: cease your advisings; for I assert, that I am superior to him in the art.

BACCHUS. Æschylus, why are you silent? for you hear his language.

EURIPIDES. He will act the dignitary at first, just as he was always accustomed to play the marvellous in his tragedies.[381]

BACCHUS. My good fellow,[382] speak not so very loftily.

EURIPIDES. I know him, and have looked him through of old—a fellow that writes savage poetry,[383] stubborn of speech, with an unbridled, licentious, unchecked tongue, unskilled in talk,[384] pomp-bundle-worded.

AESCHYLUS. Indeed? you son of the market-goddess,[385] do you say this of me,[386] you gossip-gleaner, and drawer of beggarly characters, and rag-stitcher? But by no means shall you say it with impunity.

BACCHUS. Cease, Æschylus, and do not passionately inflame your heart with wrath!

AESCHYLUS. Certainly not; before I shall have shown up clearly this introducer of lame characters, what sort[387] of a person he is, who speaks so boldly.

BACCHUS. Boys, bring out a lamb, a black lamb, for a storm[388] is ready to issue forth.

AESCHYLUS. O thou that collectest Cretan[389] monodies, and introducest unholy nuptials into the art——

BACCHUS. Hollo! stop, O highly-honoured Æschylus! And do you, O unlucky Euripides, get yourself out of the way of the hail-storm, if you are wise, lest through passion he smite your temples with a head-breaking word and let out your Telephus.[390] And do you, O Æschylus, not angrily, but temperately refute, and be refuted.[391] It is not meet that poets should rail at each other, like bread-women. But you instantly roar like a holm oak on fire.

EURIPIDES. I am ready, and do not decline, to bite, or to be bitten first, if he thinks proper, in iambics, in choral songs, and in the nerves of tragedy; and, by Jove, in the Peleus, too, and the Æolus, and the Meleager, nay, even the Telephus.[392]

BACCHUS. What, pray, do you mean to do? Tell me, Æschylus!

AESCHYLUS. I was wishing[393] not to contend here; for our contest is not on equal terms.

BACCHUS. Why, pray?

AESCHYLUS. Because[394] my poetry has not died with me, but this man’s has died with him, so that he will be able to recite it. But still, since you think proper, I must do so.

BACCHUS. Come then, let some one give me here frankincense and fire, that I may pray,[395] prior to the learned compositions, so as to decide[396] this contest most skilfully. But do you [to the Chorus] sing some song to the Muses.

CHORUS. O you chaste Muses, the nine[397] virgins of Jove, who look down upon the subtle, sagacious minds of maxim-coining men,[398] whenever they enter into competition as opponents with keenly-studied tricks of wrestling, come to observe the power of mouths most skilful in furnishing for themselves words and poetic saw-dust.[399] For now the mighty contest of skill is coming to action[400] forthwith.

BACCHUS. Now do you two also offer up some prayer, before you recite your verses.

AESCHYLUS. (offering frankincense). O Ceres, who nourished my mind, may I be[401] worthy of your mysteries!

BACCHUS. Come then, now do you also [to Euripides] offer frankincense.[402]

EURIPIDES. Excuse me; for the gods[403] to whom I pray, are different.

BACCHUS. Are they some of your own, a new[404] coinage?

EURIPIDES. Most assuredly.

BACCHUS. Come then, pray to your peculiar[405] gods.

EURIPIDES. O Air, my food,[406] and thou well-hung tongue, and sagacity, and sharp-smelling nostrils, may I rightly refute whatever arguments I assail.[407]

CHORUS. Well now, we are desirous to hear from you two learned men what hostile course of argument you will enter upon. For their tongue has been exasperated, and the spirit
of both is not devoid of courage, nor their souls sluggish. Therefore 'tis reasonable to expect that one will say something clever and well-polished; while the other, tearing them up,[408] will fall on him with words torn up from the very roots, and toss about many long rolling words.

BACCHUS. Come, you ought to recite as soon as possible: but in such manner that you shall utter what is polite, and neither metaphors,[409] nor such as any one else might say.

EURIPIDES. Well now, I will speak of myself subsequently, what I am in poetry; but first I will convict this fellow, that he was an impostor and a quack, and will show with what tricks he cajoled the spectators, having received them reared as fools in the school of Phrynichus.[410] For first of all he used to muffle up and seat some single character, an Achilles[411] or a Niobe, without showing the face, a piece of tragic quackery,[412] who did not even utter so much—
Bac. No, by Jove,[413] they certainly did not.
Eur. His chorus, on the other hand, used to hurl four series of songs one after another without ceasing; while they were silent.
Bac. But I used to like the silence, and this used to please me no less than those that chatter now-a-days.
Eur. For you were a simpleton, be well assured.
Bac. I also think so myself.[414] But why did What’s his name do this?
Eur. Out of quackery,[415] that the spectator might sit expecting, when his Niobe would[416] utter something; while the play would be going on.
Bac. O the thorough rascal! How I was cheated, then, by him! [To Æschylus.] Why are you stretching and yawning, and showing impatience?
Eur. Because I expose him. And then, when he had trifled in this way, and the drama was now half over, he used to speak some dozen words as big[417] as bulls, with brows and crests, some tremendous fellows of terrific aspect, unknown to the spectators.
Æsch. Ah me, miserable!
Bac. (to Æschylus). Be silent.
Eur. But not a single plain word would he utter.[418]
Bac. (to Æschylus). Don’t grind your teeth.
Eur. But either “Scamanders, or trenches, or griffin-eagles[419] of beaten brass upon shields,” and neck-breaking words,[420] which it was not easy to guess the meaning of.

BACCHUS. Aye, by the gods! at any rate I have lain[421] awake before now during a long space[422] of the night, trying to find out his "yellow horse-cock," what bird it is.

AESCHYLUS. It had been painted[423] as a device on the ships, you ignoramus.

BACCHUS. But I thought it was Eryxis,[424] the son of Philoxenus.

EURIPIDES. Ought you then to have introduced a cock[425] into tragedy?

AESCHYLUS. And what sort,[426] you enemy of the gods, are the things which you introduced?

EURIPIDES. Not horse-cocks, by Jove, nor yet goat-stags, as you do, such as they depict on the Persian tapestry:[427] but immediately, as soon as ever I received the art from you, puffed out with pompous phrases and ponderous words, I first of all reduced it, and took off its ponderousness with versicles, and argumentations, and with white beet,[428] giving it chatter-juice, filtering it from books: and then I nursed it up with monodies, making an infusion of Cephisophon.[429] Then I did not
trifle with whatever I met with, nor rashly[430] jumbled things together; but he who came forward first used straightway to tell the pedigree of the piece.[431]
Bac. For, by Jove, 'twas better than to tell your own.[432]
Eur. Then[433] from the first verse I used to leave nothing idle; but a woman[434] would speak for me, or a slave all the same, or a master, or a virgin, or an old woman.
Æsch. Then ought you not, pray, to have been put to death for daring to do this?
Eur. No, by Apollo; for I did it as a popular act.
Bac. No more of this, my good friend; for upon this subject your argumentation does not appear to the best advantage.[435]

AESCHYLUS. I am angry at the encounter, and my heart is indignant that[465] I must reply to this man. Yet, that he may not say I am at a loss, [to Euripides,] answer[466] me, for what ought we to admire a poet?

EURIPIDES. For cleverness and instruction, and because we make the people in the cities better.

EURIPIDES. This mischief now is spreading.[474] He will kill me with his repeated helmet-making.

BACCHUS. And by having done what did you teach them to be so noble-minded? [Æschylus is silent.] Speak, Æschylus, and do not be churlishly haughty and angry.

AESCHYLUS. By having composed a drama full of martial spirit.

BACCHUS. Of what kind?

AESCHYLUS. The “Seven against Thebes.” Every man that saw it would long to be a warrior.[475]

BACCHUS. Indeed this has been ill done of you; for you have made the Thebans[476] more courageous for the war; and for this you must be beaten.[477]

AESCHYLUS. It was in your power to practise it; but you did not turn yourselves to this. Then I published the "Persæ" after this[478] and taught them to desire always to conquer their adversaries, having embellished a most noble achievement.

BACCHUS. Of a truth I was delighted, when report was made about the defunct Darius, and the chorus immediately struck its hands together thus and exclaimed "Alas!"[479]

AESCHYLUS. This it behoves poets to practise. For observe how useful the[480] noble poets have been from of old. BACCHUS. And yet, nevertheless, he did not teach the most

AESCHYLUS. But, by Jupiter, I did not introduce harlot Phædras or Sthenobœas;[489] nor does any one know any[490] woman whom I ever represented in love.[491]

EURIPIDES. No, by Jove; for neither was there aught of Venus in you.

BACCHUS. Yea, by Jupiter, this is assuredly the case; for you have been yourself afflicted with those things, which you composed upon other men's wives.

EURIPIDES. Why, what harm, you wretched fellow, do my Sthenobœas do to the city?

AESCHYLUS. Because you have moved women, well-born, and the wives of well-born men, to drink hemlock, shamed on account of your Bellerophons.

EURIPIDES. But is this story which [494] I composed about Phædra, an unreal one?

AESCHYLUS. No, by Jove, but a real [495] one. Yet it becomes a poet to hide wickedness, and not to bring it forward, or represent it; for he who directs them is teacher to the little children, but poets to those [496] who are grown up. In truth, it greatly behoves us to speak what is useful.

AESCHYLUS. First by dressing royal personages in rags,[502] that they might appear to men to be piteous.

EURIPIDES. By doing what then have I injured in this?

AESCHYLUS. Therefore on account of this no one who is wealthy is willing to be trierarch,[503] but wraps himself in rags[504] and weeps, and declares he is poor.

For they have been soldiers, and each of them with a book[522] learns

BACCHUS. And which of his will you examine?

EURIPIDES. Very many. But first recite me that from the Oresteia.[525]

EURIPIDES. Does not Orestes then say this over the tomb of his deceased father?

AESCHYLUS. It certainly was not that one; but he addressed Mercury, the helper,[531] as "Terrestrial," and made it plain by saying that he has obtained this prerogative from his father.

EURIPIDES. (to Bacchus). Observe the expression; I will point it out to you: “For I have come to this land,” says he, “and am returning.” But “I have come,” is the same[535] with “I am returning.”

BACCHUS. Yes, by Jove, just as if one were to say to one’s neighbour, “Lend[536] me a kneading-trough, or, if you will,[537] a trough to knead in.”

AESCHYLUS. (to Bacchus). This is certainly not the same, you chattering fellow; but it is[538] a most excellent verse.

AESCHYLUS. “To have come” to a land[540] is in any one’s power who has his part in a country, for he has come to it without any calamity[541] besides; but a man in exile “comes and returns from exile.”[542]

BACCHUS. Good, by Apollo! What say you, Euripides?
