THE THESMOPHORIAZUSAE

[SCENE—the front of Agathon’s house.]

MNESILOCHUS. O JUPITER! will the swallow ever[1] appear? The man will kill me with dragging me about[2] from early dawn! Is it possible, Euripides, before I lose my spleen entirely, to learn from you whither you are leading me?

EURIPIDES. (with great seriousness). Nay, you must[3] not hear all that you will soon see, being present.

MNESILOCHUS. How say you? Tell it me again! Must I not hear?

EURIPIDES. Not what you are to see.

MNESILOCHUS. Then must I not even see?

EURIPIDES. Not what you must hear.

MNESILOCHUS. How do you advise me? Upon my word, you speak cleverly! You say I must neither hear nor see.

EURIPIDES. Not so; for, be well assured, the nature of each of them is distinct, of not hearing, and of not seeing.

MNESILOCHUS. How distinct?

EURIPIDES. Thus have these been distinguished formerly.[4] For Ether, when first it was separated,[5] and in itself bore moving animals, first contrived an eye for that which ought to see, modelled after the sun’s disk, and bored ears like a funnel.

MNESILOCHUS. On account of the funnel, then, must I[6] neither hear nor see? By Jove, I am delighted at having learned this in addition! What a thing, I ween, are learned conversazioni!

EURIPIDES. Many such matters mayest thou learn from me.

MNESILOCHUS. Would,[7] then, that, in addition to these good things, I could find out how I might still learn in addition to be lame[8] in my legs.

EURIPIDES. Come hither, and give me your attention!

MNESILOCHUS. Well!

EURIPIDES. Do you see this little door?[9]

MNESILOCHUS. Yes, by Hercules, I think so!

EURIPIDES. Be silent then![10]

MNESILOCHUS. Must I be silent about the little door?

EURIPIDES. Hear!

MNESILOCHUS. Must I hear and be silent about the little door?

EURIPIDES. Here dwells[11] the illustrious Agathon the tragic poet.

MNESILOCHUS. Of what sort[12] is this Agathon?

EURIPIDES. There is a certain Agathon——

MNESILOCHUS. Is it the black, the strong one?

EURIPIDES. No; another one. Have you never seen him?[13]

MNESILOCHUS. Is it the shaggy-bearded one?[14]

EURIPIDES. Have you never seen him?

MNESILOCHUS. Certainly not, by Jove, as far as I know![15]

EURIPIDES. And yet you have coquetted with him, but you don't know it[16] perhaps. Come, let us crouch out of the way! for a domestic of his is coming out with fire and myrtle-wreaths. He seems about to make a previous[17] sacrifice on behalf of his poetic composition. [They retire to one side.]

[coming out of the house]
SERVANT OF AGATHON. Let all the people abstain from ill-omened words, having closed their mouths; for the company of the Muses is sojourning within
my master's house, composing lyric poems. And let the breathless[18] Ether check its blasts, and the azure wave of the sea not roar—

MNESILOCHUS. Oh my !

EURIPIDES. Be silent! What are you saying?[19]
SERV.—and let the race of birds be put to sleep, and the feet of savage wild beasts that roam the woods not be put in motion.

MNESILOCHUS. Oh my gracious !

SERVANT OF AGATHON. For the beautifully-speaking Agathon our chief[20] is about—

MNESILOCHUS. To be debauched?

SERVANT OF AGATHON. Who's he that spoke?

MNESILOCHUS. Breathless Ether.

SERVANT OF AGATHON. —to lay the stocks,[21] the beginning of a drama. And he is bending new felloes for verses: others he is turning[22] on the lathe, other verses he is patching together; and he is coining maxims, and speaking in tropes,[23] and is moulding as in wax, and is rounding, and is casting—

MNESILOCHUS. And is wenching.

SERVANT OF AGATHON. What rustic[24] approaches our eaves?

MNESILOCHUS. One who is ready to turn and whirl round and cast this toe of mine in the eaves of[25] you and your beautifully-speaking poet.

SERVANT OF AGATHON. Doubtless you were a rake, old man, when you were young.[26]

EURIPIDES. My good sir, let this man go ; but do you by all means [27] call out Agathon hither to me !

SERVANT OF AGATHON. Make no entreaty ; for he himself will come out soon ; for he is beginning to make lyric poems. In truth, when it is winter, it is not easy to bend [28] the strophes, unless one come forth to the door to the sun. [Exit.]
MNEs. What then shall I do ?

EURIPIDES. Wait ; for he is coming forth. O Jove, what do you purpose [29] to do to me to-day ?
MNEs. By the gods, I wish to learn what this business is. Why do you groan ? Why are you vexed ? You ought [30] not to conceal it, being my son-in-law.

EURIPIDES. A great evil is ready kneaded for me.
MNEs. Of what kind ? [31]

EURIPIDES. On this day will be decided whether Euripides still lives [32] or is undone.
MNEs. Why, how ? For now neither the courts are about
to judge causes, nor is there a sitting of the Senate; for it is the third[33] day, the middle of the Thesmophoria.

EURIPIDES. In truth, I expect this very thing[34] even will destroy me. For the women have plotted against me, and are going to hold an assembly to-day about me in the temple of[35] Demeter and Persephone for my destruction.
MNEs. Wherefore? why, pray?[36]

EURIPIDES. Because I represent them in tragedy and speak ill of them.
MNEs. And justly too would you suffer,[37] by Neptune! But, as this is the case,[38] what contrivance have you?

EURIPIDES. To persuade Agathon the tragic poet to go to the temple of Demeter and Persephone.
MNEs. What to do? Tell me!

EURIPIDES. To sit in assembly among the women, and to speak whatever[39] is necessary in my defence.
MNEs. Openly,[40] or secretly?

EURIPIDES. Secretly, clothed in a woman’s stole.[41]

MNESILOCHUS. The device is a clever[42] one, and exceedingly in conformity with your disposition; for ours is the prize[43] for trickery. [The creaking of machinery is heard.]

EURIPIDES. Hush!

MNESILOCHUS. What’s the matter?

EURIPIDES. Agathon is coming out.

MNESILOCHUS. Why, of what sort is he?

EURIPIDES. He who is being wheeled[44] out. [The doors of the back scene are thrown open, and Agathon is wheeled in, fantastically dressed in women’s clothes.]

MNESILOCHUS. Assuredly[45] I am blind; for I don’t see any man here: I see Cyrene.[46]

EURIPIDES. Hush! He is preparing again to sing.[47]

MNESILOCHUS. “The ant’s[48] paths?” or what is he plaintively singing?
AGATH.[49] Damsels, take the torch sacred to the infernal
goddesses,[50] and, with a free country, raise[51] a shout in the dance!
CHO.[52] In honour of which of the gods[53] is the ode? Tell us then! I am readily induced to honour the gods.[54]

AGATHON. Come, then, Muse, glorify Phœbus, the drawer of the golden bow, who founded the walls[55] of the city in the land of Simois!

CHORUS. Deign to accept our most noble strains, O Phœbus, who in musical honours bearest off the sacred prize!

AGATHON. And chant the maiden dwelling in oak-grown mountains, the huntress Diana!

CHORUS. I follow, celebrating and glorifying the revered offspring of Latona, the unwedded Diana.

AGATHON. And Latona, and the notes of the lyre[56] accompanying the dances of the Phrygian graces in harmony with the foot.

CHORUS. I honour queen Latona, and the lyre, the mother of songs, with an approved masculine[57] voice; by which[58] light

AGATHON. Old man,[70] old man, I heard, indeed, the censure of your envy, but the pain I did not feel! I wear my attire in accordance with[71] my thoughts. For it behoveth a poet, conformably to the dramas which he must compose, to have his turn of mind in accordance with these. For example,[72] if one be composing female dramas, the body of the poet ought to have a participation in their manners.

MNESILOCHUS. Therefore do you mount on horseback when you compose a Phædra?[73]

AGATHON. But if one be composing male dramas, this is subsisting in the body.[74] But what we do not possess, this now is found to be all imitation.[75]

MNESILOCHUS. When therefore you compose satyric dramas, call me,[76] in order that I may actively compose poetry along with you in your rear.

AGATHON. Besides, it is unpolished [77] to see a poet boorish and rough with hair. Consider that that well-known [78] Ibycus, and Anacreon of Teos, and Alcæus, who softened down our music, wore a head-band, and practised soft Ionian airs ;[79] and that Phrynichus,—for you have certainly [80] heard him,—was both handsome himself and dressed handsomely. On this account then his dramas also were handsome : for it is [81] unavoidable that one compose similarly to one's nature.

MNESILOCHUS. On this account then Philocles,[82] as he is ugly, composes uglily ; and Xenocles,[83] as he is vile, composes vilely ; and Theognis,[84] again, as he is frigid, composes frigidly.

AGATHON. Most unavoidably ! For, assuredly, being aware of this, I paid attention to my person.

MNESILOCHUS. How, by the gods ?

EURIPIDES. Cease to abuse ! for I also was such a one, when I was his age, when I began to compose.

MNESILOCHUS. By Jove, I do not envy you your training.

EURIPIDES. Yet suffer me to tell on what account I came.

AGATHON. Say on !

EURIPIDES. Agathon, "it [85] suits a wise man, who is able briefly
to abridge many words in a proper manner." But having been smitten[86] by a new calamity, I have come to you as a suppliant.

AGATHON. In need of what?

EURIPIDES. The women purpose to destroy me to-day at the Thesmophoria, because I speak ill of them.

AGATHON. What aid then can you have from me?[87]

EURIPIDES. All; for if you secretly take your seat amongst the women, so as to seem to be a woman, and defend me, you will assuredly save[88] me: for you alone can speak in a manner worthy of me.[89]

AGATHON. How then do you not defend yourself in person?

EURIPIDES. I will tell you. In the first place, I am known; next, I am gray-headed and have a beard; while you are of a good countenance, fair,[90] shaven, with a woman's voice,[91] delicate, and comely to look at.

AGATHON. Euripides——

EURIPIDES. What's the matter?

AGATHON. Did you ever compose this verse? "You take[92] pleasure in beholding the light; and do you not think your father takes pleasure in beholding it?"

EURIPIDES. I did.

AGATHON. Don't expect then that I will undergo your misfortune for you: for I should be mad. But bear yourself what is yours, as a private matter. For it is not right to bear[93] one's calamities with artifices, but with endurance.

MNESILOCHUS. And yet you, you lewd fellow, are loose-breeched, not through words, but through endurance.

EURIPIDES. But what is it, for which you fear to go thither?

AGATHON. I should perish more miserably than you.

EURIPIDES. How?

AGATHON. How?—seeming to steal the nightly labours of the women, and to filch away the women's love.

MNESILOCHUS. "Steal," quoth'a! Nay, rather, by Jove, to be ravished! But, by Jove, the pretext is plausible.

EURIPIDES. What then? Will you do this?

AGATHON. Don't imagine it![94]

EURIPIDES. Oh thrice-unlucky! how I am undone!

MNESILOCHUS. Euripides, my dearest, my son-in-law,[95] do not abandon yourself!

EURIPIDES. How then, pray, shall I act?

MNESILOCHUS. Bid a long farewell to this fellow, and take and use me as you please.

EURIPIDES. Come then, since you give yourself up to me, strip off this garment!

MNESILOCHUS. Well now, it is on the ground. But what are you going to do to me?

EURIPIDES. To shave[96] these clean, but singe clear the parts below.

MNESILOCHUS. Well, do whatever you think fit! or I ought[97] never to have given myself up to you.

EURIPIDES. Agathon, you, of course, always carry a razor,—now lend us a razor!

AGATHON. Take it from thence yourself out of the razor-case.

EURIPIDES. (to Agathon). You are very good! [To Mnesilochus.] Sit yourself down! Puff out your right cheek! [Mnesilochus sits down and Euripides commences shaving.]

MNESILOCHUS. Ah me!

EURIPIDES. Why do you cry out? I'll put a gag in your mouth,[98] if you don't be silent.

MNESILOCHUS. Alas! woe is me! [Mnesilochus starts up and attempts to run away.]

EURIPIDES. Hollo you! whither are you running?[99]

MNESILOCHUS. To the temple of the august[100] goddesses; for, by Ceres, I will not stay here any longer,[101] being gashed!

EURIPIDES. Will you not then be ridiculous, pray, with the one half[102] of your face shaved?

MNESILOCHUS. I little care.

EURIPIDES. By the gods, by no means abandon me! Come hither! [Takes him by the arm and makes him sit down again.]

MNESILOCHUS. Ah me, miserable!

EURIPIDES. Keep quiet,[103] and lift up your head! Whither are you turning?

MNESILOCHUS. Mu! mu![104]

EURIPIDES. Why do you mutter?[105] Every thing has been accomplished well.

MNESILOCHUS. Ah me, miserable! Then I shall serve as a light-armed[106] soldier!

EURIPIDES. Don't be concerned about it; for you shall appear very comely. Do you wish to see yourself?

MNESILOCHUS. If you think fit, give me the looking-glass!

EURIPIDES. Do you see yourself?

MNESILOCHUS. No, by Jove, but Clisthenes![107]

EURIPIDES. Stand up, that I may singe[108] you; and stoop for-wards![109]

MNESILOCHUS. Ah me, miserable! I shall become a sucking pig.[110]

EURIPIDES. Let some one bring a torch or a lamp from within! [To Mnesilochus.] Bend yourself forwards! Take care now of your extremities! [Euripides begins to singe him.]

MNESILOCHUS. It shall be my care, by Jove! only that[111] I am burn-ing. Ah me, miserable! Water, water,[112] neighbours, before the flame take hold of my rump!

EURIPIDES. Be of good courage.

MNESILOCHUS. How be of good[113] courage, when I’m quite burnt up?

EURIPIDES. But you’ve no further trouble now; for you have finished the greatest part.

MNESILOCHUS. Foh! oh, what[114] soot! I have become burnt all about my rump.

EURIPIDES. Don’t be concerned! for another shall wipe it with a sponge.[115]

MNESILOCHUS. He shall weep then, whoever shall wash my breech.

EURIPIDES. Agathon, since you grudge to give yourself up to me, at any rate at least lend us a dress for this man,[116] and a girdle; for you will not say that you haven’t them.

AGATHON. Take and use them! I don’t grudge them.[117]

MNESILOCHUS. What then shall I take?

AGATHON. What? First take and put on the saffron-coloured robe.

MNESILOCHUS. (sniffing at it). By Venus, it smells sweetly of—lechery! Gird me up quickly! Now bring[118] me a girdle! [Euripides brings a girdle.]

EURIPIDES. There!

TH. Quid valeam?”

MNESILOCHUS. Come then, fit me out about the legs.

EURIPIDES. We want a head-dress and headband.[119]

AGATHON. Nay, rather, see here’s a woman’s cap[120] to put round him, which I wear by night!

EURIPIDES. By Jove, but it’s even very suitable![121]

MNESILOCHUS. Will it fit me? [Puts it on.]

AGATHON. By Jove, but it’s capital!

EURIPIDES. Bring an upper garment![122]

AGATHON. Take it from the little couch.

EURIPIDES. We want shoes.

AGATHON. Here, take mine!

MNESILOCHUS. Will they fit me? At all events you like to wear them loose.

AGATHON. Do you see[123] to this! But indeed[124] you have what you want. Let some one wheel me in as quickly as possible. [Exit Agathon.]

EURIPIDES. (surveying Mnesilochus' attire). He, though a man,[125] is now a woman in appearance. If you speak, see that you talk like a woman in your voice, well and naturally.[126]

MNESILOCHUS. I will try.

EURIPIDES. Go then!

MNESILOCHUS. No, by Apollo! unless you swear to me—

EURIPIDES. What?

MNESILOCHUS. —that you will help to deliver me with all your arts,[127] if any misfortune befall me.

EURIPIDES. "I swear then by Ether, the dwelling of Jove."[128]

MNESILOCHUS. Why rather than by the lodging of Hippocrates?[129]

EURIPIDES. I swear then by all your gods in a lump.[130]

MNESILOCHUS. Remember this then, that "your mind[131] swore. but your tongue has not sworn;" neither will I bind it by an oath.

EURIPIDES. Hasten quickly; for the signal[132] for the assembly in the temple of Ceres is exhibited; but I will be off. [Exit Euripides.]

MNESILOCHUS. Come on then, Thratta, follow me! See, Thratta,
what a quantity of smoke[133] ascends as the torches burn! Come, O very-beautiful Thesmophoræ,[134] receive me with good luck,[135] both on my entrance here, and on my return home again! Thratta, take down the box, and then take out a cake, that I may take and offer it to the two goddesses. O highly-honoured mistress, dear Demeter, and thou, Persephone, let me, possessing much, often[136] sacrifice to thee! but if not, now at least be undiscovered! and let my daughter, my pig,[137] meet with a husband who is rich, and besides, silly[138] and stupid! and let my little boy[139] have sense and understanding! Where, where shall I sit down in a good place, that I may hear the orators? Do you, Thratta, be off out of the way! for it is not permitted slaves to hear the words.
FEMALE HERALD.

HERALD. Let there be[140] solemn silence! Let there be solemn

Io Pæan! io Pæan! Let us rejoice!
CHORUS OF WOMEN KEEPING THE THESMOPHORIA.

O Jove of great renown! and thou with golden lyre, who inhabitest sacred Delos! and thou, all powerful damsel, gray-eyed, with spear of gold, who inhabitest a desirable city, come hither! and thou of many names, damsel slaying wild beasts, offspring of golden-eyed Latona! and thou marine, august Neptune, lord of the sea, having left thy fishy, storm-vexed[149] recess! and ye daughters of marine Nereus! and ye moun-
tain-roaming nymphs! And let the golden lyre accompany our prayers; and may we well-born Athenian women bring our debates to an accomplishment.[150]

HERALD. Pray to the Olympic gods and to the Olympic goddesses,[151] and to the Pythian gods and to the Pythian goddesses, and to the Delian gods and to the Delian goddesses, and to the other deities; if any one plots any evil[152] against the people of the women, or makes proposals of peace to Euripides[153] and the Persians for the purpose of any injury to the women, or purposes to be a tyrant,[154] or to join in bringing back the tyrant, or has denounced a woman as substituting a child, or if any woman's female slave, being a go-between, has whispered the matter in her master's ear, or if any, when sent, brings lying messages, or if any paramour deceives by telling falsehoods, and does not give what he shall have formerly promised, or if any old woman[155] gives presents to a paramour, or even if a mistress receives presents, betraying her friend, and if any male or female publican[156] falsifies the legal measure of the gallon or the half-pint, pray that he may perish miserably, himself and family, but pray that the gods may give many blessings[157] to all the rest of you.

CHORUS. We offer our united prayers that these wishes may come to be accomplished for the state, and accomplished for the people; and that those women who give[158] the best advice (as many as this befalls) may get their opinions passed. But as many as for the sake of gain deceive, and violate the established oaths for the purpose of injury, or seek to revolutionize decrees and law, and tell our secrets to our enemies, or bring in the Persians for the purpose of injury to the country, act wickedly and injure the state. But, O all-powerful Jove, mayest thou accomplish this, so that the gods stand by us, although[159] we are women.

HERALD. Hear, every one! [Unfolds a paper and begins to read the preliminary decree.] "These things have been determined on by the Senate of the women: Timoclea was Epistates,[160] Lysilla was secretary, Sostrata moved the decree; to convene an assembly in the morning[161] in the middle of the Thesmophoria, when we are most at leisure; and to debate first about Euripides, what he ought to suffer; for he has been adjudged[162] guilty by us all." FIRST WOMAN. I.

HERALD. Then first put on this crown[164] before you speak. [To the meeting.] Be silent! Be quiet! Give[165] attention! for she is now expectorating, as the orators do. She seems to be going to make a long speech.

FIRST WOMAN. Through no[166] ostentatiousness, by the two goddesses, have I stood up to speak, O women; but indeed I have been vexed, unhappy woman, now for a long time, seeing you treated with contumely by Euripides the son of the herb-woman,[167] and abused with much abuse[168] of every kind. For what abuse does he not[169] smear upon us? And where has he not calumniated us, where, in short,[170] are spectators, and tragic actors, and choruses? calling us adulteresses in disposition, lovers of the men, wine-bibbers,[171] traitresses, gossips, masses of wickedness, great pests to men. So that, as soon as[172] they come in from the wooden-benches, they look askance at us, and straightway search, lest[173] any paramour be concealed in the house. And we are no longer able to do any of those things which we formerly did: such badness has he taught our husbands. So that, if even any woman weave a crown,[174] she is thought to be in love; and if she let fall any vessel while roaming about the house, her husband asks her, "In whose honour is the pot broken? It must be for the Corinthian[175] stranger."
Is any girl [176] sick ; straightway her brother says, "This colour in the girl does not please me." Well ; does any woman, lacking children, wish to substitute a child ; it is not possible even for this to go undiscovered ; for now the husbands sit down beside [177] them. And he has calumniated us to the old men, who heretofore used to marry girls ; so that no old man is willing to marry a woman, on account of this verse, "For [178] a woman is ruler over an old bridegroom." EURIPIDES. “O wretched! whither have we sailed!”

SEVENTH WOMAN. Do you believe this fellow at all—the devil take[412] him—talking nonsense? This is the Thesmophorium.

EURIPIDES. “Is Proteus himself[413] within, or out of sight?”

SEVENTH WOMAN. It must be that you are[414] still sea-sick, stranger, who having heard that Proteus is dead, then[415] ask if he is within, or out of sight.

EURIPIDES. “Alas, he is dead! Where has he been buried in the tomb?”

MNESILOCHUS. “This is his tomb,[416] upon which I am sitting.”

SEVENTH WOMAN. Then[417] may you perish miserably! and certainly indeed you will perish, who have the impudence to call the altar a tomb.

EURIPIDES. “Why,[418] pray, do you sit in these sepulchral seats covered[419] with a veil, O female stranger?”

MNESILOCHUS. “I am [420] forced to mingle in wedlock with the son of Proteus.”

SEVENTH WOMAN. Wherefore, you wretch, are you again deceiving the stranger? [To Euripides.] This fellow, O stranger, acting the knave, came up hither to the women for the stealing of the gold.

MNESILOCHUS. “Bark away, assailing [421] me with censure.”

EURIPIDES. “Female stranger, who is the old woman who reviles [422] you?”

MNESILOCHUS. “This is Theonoe, daughter of Proteus.”

SEVENTH WOMAN. No, by the two goddesses! unless [423] Critylla daughter of Antitheus of Gargettus be so. But you’re a knave.

MNESILOCHUS. “Say whatever you please. For I will never marry [424] your brother, having abandoned [425] Menelaus, my husband, in Troy.”

EURIPIDES. “What say you, woman? Turn your sparkling eyes [426] towards mine.”

MNESILOCHUS. “I am ashamed before you, having been mauled [427] in my cheeks.”

EURIPIDES. “What’s this? Speechlessness [428] possesses me. Ye gods, what sight [429] do I behold? Who art thou, woman?”

MNESILOCHUS. "And who are you? for the same[430] word holds you and me."

EURIPIDES. "Are you a Grecian woman, or a woman of this country?"

MNESILOCHUS. "A Grecian woman. But I also wish to learn[431] yours."

EURIPIDES. "I see you very like to Helen, woman."

MNESILOCHUS. "And I you to Menelaus,[432] as far as may be judged from the pot-herbs."

EURIPIDES. "Then[433] you rightly recognise a most unfortunate man."

MNESILOCHUS. "O thou who hast come late to the arms of thy wife! Take me, take me, husband! Throw thy arms[434] around me! Come, let me kiss you! Take and lead me away, lead me away, lead me away, lead me away[435] very quickly."

SEVENTH WOMAN. Then, by the two goddesses, he shall weep,[436] whoever shall lead you away, being beaten with the torch.

EURIPIDES. "Do you hinder me from leading my wife, the daughter of Tyndareus, to Sparta?"

SEVENTH WOMAN. Ah me, what[437] a knave you also appear to me to be, and this man's counsellor! No wonder you were acting the Egyptian[438] this long while. But he shall suffer punishment; for the Prytanis is approaching, and the Policeman. [Goes towards them.]

EURIPIDES. This is unlucky. Well, I must sneak away.[439]

MNESILOCHUS. But what shall I do, unhappy man?

EURIPIDES. Remain quiet; for I will never abandon you, if I live;[440] unless my innumerable artifices fail me. [Exit Euripides.]

MNESILOCHUS. This line[441] has drawn up nothing. [Enter Prytanis and Policeman.]

PRYTANIS. Is this the knave of whom Clisthenes spoke to us? Ho you, why do you hang down[442] your head? Lead him[443] within, Policeman, and bind him to the plank, and then place him here and guard him, and suffer no one to approach him; but beat them with your whip, if any approach.

SEVENTH WOMAN. Yes, by Jove! for now assuredly[444] a tricky fellow[445] almost took him away from me.

MNESILOCHUS. O Prytanis, by your right hand, which you are accustomed to hold[446] out bent, if any one offer you money, grant me a small favour, although about to die.

PRYTANIS. In what shall I oblige[447] you?

MNESILOCHUS. Order the Policeman to strip me naked and fasten me to the plank; in order that, being an old man, I may not in saffron-coloured robes, and a woman's night-cap, afford laughter to the crows, while I feast them.[448]

PRYTANIS. It has been determined by the Senate to bind you with them on, in order that you may be clearly seen by the passers-by[449] to be a knave. [Exit Prytanis.]

MNESILOCHUS. Oh my ! oh my ! O saffron robe, what things you have done ! No longer is there any hope of safety. [Policeman leads Mnesilochus within.]
CHO.[450] Come now, let us sport, as is here the custom with the women, whenever on holy seasons we celebrate the solemn orgies of the two goddesses, which Pauson[451] also honours, and fasts, oftentimes protesting to them from season to season that such are frequently a care to himself.[452] Put yourself in motion, each of you, advance, come on lightly with your feet in a circle,[453] join hand to hand, move to the time[454] of the dance ; go with swift feet. MNESILOCHUS. O Policeman, I beseech you!

POLICEMAN. Don’t beseech me!

MNESILOCHUS. Loosen the nail.

POLICEMAN. Well, I am[466] doing so. [Hammers it in tighter.]

MNESILOCHUS. Ah me, miserable! you are hammering it in the more.[467]

POLICEMAN. Do you wish[468] it to be hammered still more?

MNESILOCHUS. Alas, alas! May you perish miserably.

POLICEMAN. Be silent, miserable old man! Come, let me bring a mat,[469] in order that I may guard you. [Goes out and returns again with a mat.]

MNESILOCHUS. These are the blessed fruits which[470] I have enjoyed from Euripides. Ha, ye gods, preserver Jove, there are hopes! The man does not seem likely to abandon[471] me; but he ran forth as[472] Perseus, and secretly gave me a sign that I

Eur. Women, if you are willing to make[531] peace with me for the future, it is now in your power; I make you these proposals of peace on the understanding[532] that you are to be in no wise abused by me at all henceforth.

CHORUS. On account[533] of what matter do you bring forward this proposal?
Eur. This man in the plank is my father-in-law. If therefore I recover him, you shall never[534] be abused at all. But if you do not comply, I will accuse you to your husbands when they come home from the army of those things which you do secretly.[535]

CHORUS. For our[536] parts, be assured that we are prevailed upon. But this barbarian you must[537] prevail upon yourself.
Eur. That is my business; and yours, [turning to the dancing-girl,] Elaphium, is to remember to do what I told you on the road. In the first place therefore walk past him,[538] and gird yourself up. And do you, [turning to the boy,] Teredon,[539] play an accompaniment to the Persian[540] dance.

POLICEMAN. Yes, yes, sit down, sit down, yes, yes, my little daughter. [Dancing-girl sits down upon the Policeman's knee.] Ah, how firm[544] her breast is, like a turnip.

POLICEMAN. Beauteous she is behind! You shall repent, if you do not remain within. Well! beauteous she is before.

EURIPIDES. It is well. Take your dress: it is time for us now to go.

POLICEMAN. Will she not kiss me first?

EURIPIDES. Certainly. [To the dancing-girl.] Kiss him! [She kisses him.]

POLICEMAN. Oh, oh, oh! Oh my! How sweet her lips! like Attic honey. Why does she not remain with me?

EURIPIDES. Farewell, Policeman! for this cannot be.

POLICEMAN. Yes, yes, old woman, gratify me in this.

EURIPIDES. Then will you give me a drachma?

POLICEMAN. Yes, yes, I'll give it you.

EURIPIDES. Then bring the money.

POLICEMAN. But I have not any.[545] Come, take my quiver.

EURIPIDES. Then you'll bring her again.

POLICEMAN. (to the dancing-girl). Follow me, my child! And do you, old woman, guard the old man. — But what's your name?

EURIPIDES. Artemisia. Therefore remember my name.

POLICEMAN. Artamuxia. [Exit Policeman with the dancing-girl.]
